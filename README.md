# Data_management_Project
Building a Global Climate Vulnerability Index: Measuring Exposure, Sensitivity, and Adaptive Capacity Across Countries - By Valentine PETZOLD and Clémence HUANG

Climate change acts as a “threat multiplier,” intensifying existing inequalities and generating new risks across countries. Its impacts are highly uneven: similar physical shocks, such as heatwaves or floods, can have dramatically different consequences depending on national contexts. These differences are driven not only by geography, but also by socio-economic and institutional conditions. Against this background, the objective of this project is to construct a Climate Vulnerability Index, based on the framework commonly used in IPCC reports, which defines vulnerability as a function of exposure, sensitivity, and adaptive capacity.

By combining these dimensions using transparent data and methods, the project seeks to identify the main drivers of climate vulnerability and to distinguish country profiles that may require differentiated policy responses. This leads to the following research question: **How can a composite climate vulnerability index be used to compare countries and support more targeted and equitable climate adaptation policies ?**

To operationalize the index, vulnerability is decomposed into three sub-dimensions. Exposure captures the physical reality of climate change and is considered largely exogenous, as countries cannot alter their geographical exposure to natural hazards. Temperature change is selected as the main indicator, serving as a proxy for increasing thermal stress and related climate risks.

Sensitivity reflects the social dimension of vulnerability, defined as the extent to which a country’s socio-economic structure amplifies the damage caused by climate shocks. This dimension is measured through socio-economic fragility. The Human Development Index (HDI) is included, as higher human development is generally associated with stronger institutions and more effective crisis management. Income inequality is also incorporated, since more equal societies tend to display higher social cohesion, facilitating collective action and information diffusion during climate-related events.

Adaptive capacity represents the institutional ability to cope with and adjust to climate impacts. This is the dimension most directly influenced by public policy. Environmental Protection Expenditures are used to capture the financial commitment of governments to climate and environmental issues, while the share of renewable energy reflects technological capacity and long-term resilience through reduced dependence on fossil fuels.

While this composite index provides valuable insights, it is subject to limitations. Data gaps, indicator selection, and equal weighting assumptions may influence results, and normalization methods can amplify the effect of outliers. Despite these constraints, the CVI offers a transparent, reproducible framework for assessing vulnerability and supporting evidence-based decision-making.
